** Introduction
<<cap.intro>>

High-Performance Computing (HPC) platforms combine multi-core CPUs and
GPUs, often in nodes of different generations and architectures. Programming such
platforms directly, deciding where each computation runs and when each
data transfer happens, is complex and does not port well from one
platform to another. To mitigate that problem, it is appeal to use Task-based programming, which addresses this problem: the
application is expressed as a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) of tasks
with their data dependencies, and a runtime system, such as StarPU
\cite{augonnet2011starpu} and PaRSEC (citar parsec), maps the tasks to the resources, transfers
the data, and balances the load at execution time. The programmer still
decides, however, which resources the application uses, and in
particular on how many nodes it runs.

This decision is harder for applications made of phases with different
computational profiles. ExaGeoStat \cite{abdulah2018exageostat}, a
geostatistics application, is an example. Each iteration of its
Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) first generates a covariance
matrix, an embarrassingly parallel phase that runs on CPUs, and then
factorizes it with a Cholesky factorization, a phase with many
dependencies and communications that benefits from GPUs. On a
heterogeneous platform, adding nodes speeds up the generation but may
slow down the factorization, because the extra nodes add
communications and are slower than the GPU nodes. Using all nodes in
both phases, the default choice, is therefore not necessarily the
fastest.

\citeonline{nesi2021icpp} exploited this difference. They removed the
synchronization between the two phases, so that the factorization
starts while the generation is still running, and gave each phase its
own data distribution, so that each phase uses the nodes more suitable
to it: the generation runs on all nodes and the factorization on a
subset of them. Since the best number of factorization nodes depends
on the platform and on the problem, \citeonline{nesi2022ipdps} then
proposed to learn it online, during the iterations of a single
execution, with a Gaussian process guided by a lower bound computed by
a linear program. In 16 platform scenarios, this strategy found good
configurations in few iterations and reached a speedup of up to 51.2%
over using all nodes in both phases.

Three questions remain open after that work. First, the selection has
only one dimension: the generation always uses all nodes, although the
number of generation nodes could also be chosen. Second, the objective
is only time. With the per-phase execution, some nodes stay idle during
a phase, and configurations with similar time may use different
numbers of nodes and thus consume different amounts of energy. Third,
the implementation is a patch on the legacy version of ExaGeoStat, on
top of versions of StarPU and Chameleon that are no longer maintained,
and the current version of the application, ExaGeoStatCPP, does not
include the per-phase execution.

This work addresses these three questions. Its main objective is to
learn online, during a single execution of a multi-phase task-based
application, how many nodes each phase should use, with energy
consumption as part of the objective. The expected contributions are:
(i) the per-phase execution of ExaGeoStat on the current software
stack, packaged for reproducibility; (ii) an online strategy that
selects jointly the number of generation and factorization nodes; and
(iii) the inclusion of energy in this selection, with measurements on
real hardware.

The remainder of this document is organized as follows. Section
[[sec.motivation]] quantifies, from the data published by
\citeonline{nesi2022ipdps}, how much the second dimension brings and
why energy matters for it. Chapter [[cap.objectives]] details the
objectives and the expected results. Chapter [[cap.related]] discusses
the related work. Chapter [[cap.methodology]] presents the
methodology, and Section [[sec.schedule]] the schedule.

*** Motivation
<<sec.motivation>>

The online selection of \citeonline{nesi2022ipdps} fixes the
generation on all nodes and learns only the number of factorization
nodes. Their Figure 8, however, explores both dimensions for one
scenario, named (f) G5K 2L-6M-15S 128: it presents the simulated
iteration time of every pair (generation nodes, factorization nodes)
with at least as many generation nodes as factorization nodes, on a
platform of 23 heterogeneous Grid'5000 nodes of three types.
The authors observed that, in
this scenario, 10 generation nodes and 8 factorization nodes are
about 3% faster than the best configuration with the generation on
all nodes. They concluded that, although the Gaussian process should
extend to more dimensions, the gain would be limited in practice, and
left the modeling of the two-dimensional space as future work. We
revisited this data to look beyond the best pair. Figure
\ref{fig.motivation} summarizes the
result.

Energy breaks this tie. If the nodes that no phase uses are released,
generating on 9 nodes instead of 16 uses 7 fewer nodes for practically
the same iteration time. Even if the nodes stay reserved, idle nodes
typically consume less power than active ones. The configurations that
time cannot distinguish thus differ in energy, which is why the joint
selection of the number of nodes of both phases is relevant when energy
is part of the objective. The thesis of \citeonline{nesi2023thesis}
already measured time and energy on Grid'5000 Lille for configurations that vary the number
of generation and factorization nodes of each node type, and presented
the time-energy Pareto front of these configurations. This is the
closest previous result to the energy part of this work.


This analysis has limitations. It covers a single scenario, with one
simulated value per pair, so the variability of real executions is
absent.
In addition, two of the three node types of this scenario are no
longer available in Grid'5000, so the scenario can only be reproduced
in simulation. Characterizing when the second dimension matters, in
time and in energy, on current platforms is part of the objectives of
this work.

** Objectives
<<cap.objectives>>

The main objective of this work is to learn online, during a single
execution of a multi-phase task-based application, how many nodes each
phase should use, with energy consumption as part of the objective.
We use the ExaGeoStat geostatistics application as the case study.
Each iteration of its Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) has two
phases with different profiles: the generation of the covariance
matrix and its Cholesky factorization. The selection is made jointly
for both phases, the number of nodes for the generation and the number
of nodes for the factorization, while the application runs. This
extends the one-dimensional selection of \citeonline{nesi2022ipdps},
in which the generation always uses all nodes. To reach this goal, we
define three specific objectives:

- Implement the asynchronous execution of the two phases and a
  separate data distribution for each phase in ExaGeoStatCPP, the
  current version of ExaGeoStat, on top of the latest StarPU and Chameleon
  versions, with the whole stack packaged in Nix for reproducibility.
-  Design and
  evaluate online strategies that choose the pair (generation nodes,
  factorization nodes) along the MLE iterations, with the Gaussian
  process approach of \citeonline{nesi2022ipdps} as the starting
  point.
-  Extend the selection so that
  it minimizes energy consumption under a time constraint
  $T \leq (1+\varepsilon)\,T^{*}$, where $T^{*}$ is the best
  iteration time.

*** Expected Results

At the end of this work, we expect to provide an up-to-date and
reproducible version of the per-phase execution of ExaGeoStat, an
online method that selects the resources of each phase, and a
quantification of the time and energy gains of this selection
compared to the one-dimensional selection and to using all nodes.

** Methodology
<<cap.methodology>>

The work follows the order of the specific objectives. We first bring
the per-phase execution of \citeonline{nesi2021icpp} to the current
software stack, then use it to study the online selection of the
number of nodes of both phases, and finally add energy to the
objective. This section describes the application and its
runtime (Section [[sec.exageostat]]), our previous work on which this
plan builds (Section [[sec.previous]]), and the current state of the
software (Section [[sec.stack]]). Section [[sec.schedule]] presents the
schedule.

*** ExaGeoStat on Chameleon and StarPU
<<sec.exageostat>>

StarPU \cite{augonnet2011starpu} is a task-based runtime that uses the
Sequential Task Flow (STF) model. The application submits tasks
sequentially, declaring the data each task reads and writes, and the
runtime infers the dependencies, builds the Directed Acyclic Graph
(DAG) of tasks, and schedules each task to a CPU core or a GPU at
execution time. The runtime also transfers data between memories and
nodes, so that the same code runs on heterogeneous platforms. With
StarPU-MPI, each node submits the whole DAG, executes the tasks that
write data it owns, and exchanges data with the other nodes when a
dependency crosses nodes. The data distribution, that is, which node
owns each tile of a matrix, therefore defines where each task runs.
Chameleon \cite{agullo2010chameleon} is a dense linear algebra library
built on this model, which provides, among others, the tiled Cholesky
factorization.

ExaGeoStat \cite{abdulah2018exageostat} is a geostatistics application
that estimates the parameters of a spatial statistical model by
Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE). Each MLE iteration evaluates the
likelihood for a set of parameters proposed by an optimizer. The
evaluation has two dominant phases with different profiles. The
generation phase builds the covariance matrix: each tile is computed
independently from the locations and the parameters, which makes the
phase embarrassingly parallel and executed on CPUs. The factorization
phase computes the Cholesky factorization of this matrix with
Chameleon, which has many dependencies, communicates between nodes, and
benefits from GPUs. Because of these differences, the number of nodes
that minimizes the time of one phase is not necessarily the one that
minimizes the time of the other.

\citeonline{nesi2021icpp} exploit this difference in two ways. First,
the phases run asynchronously: the factorization of a tile starts as
soon as the tile is generated, without waiting for the end of the
generation. Second, each phase has its own data distribution: the
generation uses all nodes, the factorization uses a subset of them,
and the tiles migrate between the two distributions.
\citeonline{nesi2022ipdps} then learn the number of factorization
nodes online, along the MLE iterations, with a Gaussian process guided
by a lower bound computed by a linear program. This work keeps this execution model and adds the number of
generation nodes as a second dimension of the selection.

*** Software stack and current state
<<sec.stack>>


The implementation of \citeonline{nesi2022ipdps} is a patch on the
legacy version of ExaGeoStat, written in C, on top of versions of
StarPU and Chameleon that are no longer maintained. The current version
of the application, ExaGeoStatCPP 2.0, was rewritten in C++ and is
pinned to StarPU 1.3.10 and Chameleon 1.1.0, but does not include the
per-phase execution: the generation is followed by a wait for all its
tasks before the factorization starts, the factorization uses the
synchronous Chameleon interface, and there is neither a custom data
distribution nor migration between phases. Chameleon already provides
the building blocks for these features. It has an asynchronous
interface, supports custom data distributions, and, since version 1.2,
provides a function to change the distribution of a matrix
asynchronously, which is equivalent to the data migration implemented
in the patch, but only with the StarPU backend. Moving to the latest
Chameleon, version 1.4, also requires StarPU 1.4. Chameleon 1.4.0
already has a Nix package, and we packaged ExaGeoStatCPP in Nix in our
fork, which is the base of this work.


*** Schedule
<<sec.schedule>>


Table \ref{tab.sched} presents the proposed schedule, from December
2026 until the defense in March 2028. The first step is to bring the
per-phase execution to ExaGeoStatCPP, testing it first on a single node
and then on multiple nodes of the PCAD cluster, and to validate it by
reproducing the one-dimensional gain of \citeonline{nesi2022ipdps} on
Grid'5000 Lille. Meanwhile, two-dimensional strategies are compared in
the resampling simulator of \citeonline{nesi2022ipdps}, and the most
promising ones are then evaluated online on real executions. Next, we
measure the energy consumption of the configurations on Grid'5000
Lille and extend the online selection to the energy objective. The
last steps are a journal article with the consolidated results and the
writing of the master thesis. If the schedule slips, the energy part is
reduced to an offline characterization, and the legacy implementation
of \citeonline{nesi2022ipdps} can replace the port as the base for the
online selection.
